Zeroing

Zeroing

Zeroing means adjusting the scope so the rifle hits where you aim – dead on or with a hunting zero. Procedure: shoot a group, find the mean point of impact, turn the correction in clicks, fire a confirmation shot. Always use the ammunition, suppressor and rest you will later shoot with.

Point of aim and point of impact

The point of aim is where the reticle points at the shot, the point of impact is where the bullet lands. When zeroing you always aim at the same spot, usually the centre of the crosshair on the target. The offset between point of aim and mean point of impact is what you correct.

Dead-on and high zero

Dead on means point of impact equals point of aim at the zeroing distance. A high zero means the rifle deliberately hits a little high, typically 4 cm at 100 m. The trajectory then stays within the vital zone over a longer range, see optimal zero range.

Optimal zero range (GEE)

The German “GEE” is the distance at which a rifle hits dead on when the trajectory never rises more than 4 cm above the line of sight on the way. In practice you zero 4 cm high at 100 m; with common hunting calibres the zero is then roughly at 150 to 200 m. Up to a little beyond that the hit stays within ±4 cm, so you can shoot without holdover. How far exactly depends on muzzle velocity, BC and sight height.

Group

Several shots with the same point of aim and unchanged settings. A single shot says little because every rifle disperses. Three shots are the minimum, five are much more meaningful. Let the barrel cool between groups, see barrel heat.

Group size

The largest distance between two hits of a group, measured centre to centre. It describes the precision of rifle, ammunition and shooter together. Expressed as an angle (MOA or mrad) groups at different distances can be compared.

Mean point of impact

The centre of all hits of a group. You always correct towards it, never towards a single shot. Its offset from the target point in centimetres, divided by the click value at that distance, gives the number of clicks.

Flyer

A hit that lies clearly outside the others. Typical causes: clean-bore shot, jerking the trigger, a gust of wind, the rifle touching the rest. A flyer with a known cause should not go into the correction; if flyers keep happening, the problem lies with rifle, mount or ammunition.

Confirmation shot

A shot after adjusting to confirm the correction. It should land inside the group size around the new target point. If it is far off although the rifle was steady, check turning direction, click value and mount.

Clean-bore shot and cold bore

The first shot from a freshly cleaned or oiled barrel often hits differently from the following ones: oil residue and bare steel change friction and gas pressure. So dry the bore after cleaning and fire one or two fouling shots that don’t count before zeroing. Related is the cold-bore shot: the first shot from a cold barrel. For hunting this is exactly the one that matters – check now and then where the first shot of the day lands.

Riflescope

Parts of a riflescope

At the front the objective, at the back the eyepiece with diopter adjustment, in between the main tube (usually 30 or 34 mm) with the turrets: elevation on top, windage on the right, parallax on the left on many models. The magnification ring sets the zoom. Where the reticle sits in the optical path decides the focal plane.

MOA – minute of angle

A minute of angle is 1/60 of a degree. At 100 m that is 2.91 cm, at 200 m 5.82 cm – the size grows with distance. Scopes with MOA adjustment usually have 1/4 MOA per click, about 0.73 cm at 100 m. Some makers use “inch per 100 yards” (2.78 cm at 100 m), slightly less than a true MOA.

mrad – milliradian

One thousandth of a radian. At 100 m that is 10 cm, at 300 m 30 cm. With 0.1 mrad per click one click moves 1 cm at 100 m – easy mental maths. 1 mrad equals 3.44 MOA. What matters is that reticle and turrets use the same unit.

Click value

How far one click on the turret moves the point of impact, as an angle. Common are 0.1 mrad (1 cm at 100 m) or 1/4 MOA (0.73 cm at 100 m). Formula: clicks = offset in cm ÷ (click value in cm at 100 m × distance ÷ 100). Example: 6 cm low at 100 m with 0.1 mrad → 6 clicks up.

Turrets, direction and zero reset

The elevation turret moves the point of impact up or down, the windage turret right or left. The arrows (U/Up, R) show which way the point of impact moves. After zeroing, many turrets let you reset the scale to zero without changing the setting. A zero stop prevents turning below the zeroed value.

First and second focal plane

In the first focal plane (FFP) the reticle grows with magnification, so its spacings (e.g. 1 mrad between dots) are correct at every magnification. In the second focal plane (SFP) the reticle always stays the same size while the target grows; spacings are only correct at one magnification, usually the highest. For driven hunts many prefer SFP because the reticle stays clearly visible at low magnification.

Reticle

The aiming mark in the scope. Common in German-speaking countries is the No. 4 reticle: three thick posts and a fine crosshair in the centre. An illuminated dot helps at dusk. Reticles with mrad or MOA marks (e.g. Mil-Dot) allow holdover and range estimation, provided focal plane and magnification match.

Parallax

If the target image and the reticle are not in the same plane, the reticle moves on the target when you move your head. Hunting scopes are usually parallax-free at 100 m. At other distances a parallax adjustment helps, or a consistent head position with the eye centred behind the eyepiece.

Eye relief

The distance between eyepiece and eye at which the image is fully lit, usually 8 to 10 cm. If it is too short, recoil can cut the eyebrow (“scope bite”). Mount the scope so that in shooting position you get a full, shadow-free image without straining your neck.

Mount and sight height

The mount connects scope and rifle. Tighten screws to the torque the maker specifies – too loose and the point of impact wanders, too tight and the tube can suffer. Sight height is the distance between bore axis and scope centre. It decides how the trajectory crosses the line of sight and belongs in every ballistic calculator.

Ballistics & conditions

Muzzle velocity

The speed of the bullet at the muzzle in m/s. It depends on the load, barrel length and powder temperature. Higher velocity means a flatter trajectory and less wind drift. Factory figures apply to a test barrel; your own barrel can differ noticeably.

Ballistic coefficient (BC)

A measure of how well a bullet overcomes air resistance. The higher the BC, the slower the bullet loses speed, the flatter the trajectory and the less wind drift. Values are given for different models (G1, G7); only compare values of the same model.

Air density and the ideal gas law

Air resistance depends on air density. By the ideal gas law ρ = p ÷ (Rs · T) with Rs = 287 J/(kg·K) for dry air. At 15 °C and 1013 hPa that is 1.225 kg/m³. Warmer or higher up means thinner air: about 5 % less at 30 °C, about 10 % less at 1000 m altitude. The bullet slows down less and hits higher at long range. At 100 m this hardly matters, from about 300 m it does.

Powder temperature

Warm powder burns faster, raising pressure and muzzle velocity. Depending on the powder, velocity changes by roughly 0.5 to 1.5 m/s per degree. If you zero in summer and hunt in winter, check before the season. Don’t leave cartridges in direct sun or on a hot barrel.

Wind

Crosswind pushes the bullet sideways. What matters is not the flight time but the delay compared with flight in a vacuum: drift ≈ wind speed × (flight time − distance ÷ muzzle velocity). Example .308 Win at about 820 m/s with 3 m/s full-value wind: roughly 1.5 cm at 100 m, roughly 13 cm at 300 m. With the clock method, wind from 3 or 9 o’clock is full value, from 1, 5, 7 and 11 o’clock about half, from 2, 4, 8 and 10 o’clock almost full. Choose a calm day for zeroing.

Uphill and downhill

When shooting steeply up or down, gravity only acts over the horizontal distance. The bullet therefore hits higher than the measured slant range suggests. Rule of thumb: use the horizontal distance (slant range × cos angle). At 30° that is 87 %.

Barrel & ammunition

Barrel and barrel harmonics

When firing, the barrel whips like a lash. Where it is in that motion when the bullet leaves the muzzle decides the point of impact. Anything that changes the vibration shifts the point of impact: a different load, a suppressor, pressure on the barrel, extra weight. A free-floating barrel doesn’t touch the stock and shoots more consistently. That is also why the smartphone never goes on the barrel.

Barrel heat and mirage

With every shot the barrel gets warmer; many barrels shift their point of impact as they heat up. Hot air above the barrel shimmers and distorts the image. Let the barrel cool between groups until it only feels lukewarm to the touch. With thin hunting barrels this happens sooner than you think.

Twist

The rifling spins the bullet and stabilises it. The twist rate states the distance over which the bullet makes one full turn, e.g. 1:11″ = one turn in 11 inches (279 mm). Long, heavy bullets need a faster (shorter) twist; an old rule of thumb for this is the Greenhill formula. With right-hand twist the bullet drifts slightly right at long range (spin drift) – at 100 m only millimetres.

Barrel length

A longer barrel gives the powder more time to accelerate the bullet: more muzzle velocity, a slightly flatter trajectory. How much depends strongly on the calibre – little for short cartridges, more for magnums. Shorter barrels are handier and popular with a suppressor.

Suppressor

A suppressor changes weight and vibration at the end of the barrel and so usually the point of impact, often by several centimetres. Always zero in the configuration you hunt with, and check again after removing and refitting it.

Load and lot

The load is the exact make-up of a cartridge: bullet, weight, powder, charge. Every load has its own point of impact, so re-zero after a change. A lot is a production batch; even lots of the same cartridge can differ slightly. If you want to be precise, buy a season’s ammunition from one lot.
